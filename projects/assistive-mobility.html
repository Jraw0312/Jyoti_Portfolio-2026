Assistive Mobility in Indian Homes

Master's thesis on domestic mobility barriers for people with lower-limb disabilities in Indian homes. Used participatory and co-speculative design to surface frictions that direct questioning misses. Four phases. 32 participants. Ended in two 3D-printed concepts tested inside a VR home built in Unity.

ROLE

M.Des Thesis · Design Research · Participatory HCI · Prototyping
HCI ACCESSIBILITY ASSISTIVE TECH DISABILITY STUDIES DOMESTIC MOBILITY

Can assistive technology be designed from inside the home?

That was the question behind this thesis. I was interested in mobility not only as a device problem, but as a domestic interaction problem — shaped by bodies, floors, thresholds, bathrooms, furniture, family support, and everyday routines.

The work focused on people with lower-limb disabilities in Indian domestic contexts, where accessibility is often negotiated through jugaad, care, adaptation, and compromise.

The problem

Assistive mobility devices are usually designed, prescribed, or evaluated outside the home. A wheelchair tested in a hospital corridor works fine there and fails at a bathroom threshold. The literature on assistive technology abandonment backs this up: devices get rejected not because they're broken, but because they don't fit the person, the task, or the space.

The harder problem was methodological. In early interviews, people didn't describe their daily friction as a problem. They described it as something they'd adapted to. Leaning on a wall, calling a family member, avoiding a room. A barrier you've already built a routine around stops sounding like a barrier when someone asks you about it directly.

So the real question became: how do you get someone to notice a problem they've stopped noticing.

FIELDWORK / DOMESTIC FRICTIONS

01 / 03

What I studied

I worked through interviews, photographs, participatory workshops, scenario animations, sketches, CAD models, 3D prints, and VR simulations. Each method helped surface a different layer of access, dependence, comfort, and movement.

  • Phase 1, semi-structured interviews 9 participants, recruited purposively at a community health centre in Ghaziabad. Surfaced four initial problem areas: bathroom access and threshold crossing, movement through confined or crowded space, kitchen reach, and adaptation via avoidance or assistance. Two participants contributed photo-elicitation material from their own homes voluntarily.
  • Phase 2, bespoke booklet workshop I 14 participants at the Indian Spinal Injuries Centre, Delhi. A bilingual booklet built from the Phase 1 photos and findings, with space to write, mark, or sketch. Worked well for critique, weak for generating new ideas. Participants spent more time on cost, policies and government support than on imagining future devices.
  • Phase 3, situated co-speculative artifact based workshop II 8 participants, same site. Swapped static images for two short animations: moving from the living room to the bathroom, and to the kitchen. Showing movement instead of describing it got people talking about wheel size, footrests, seat height, and the fear of falling at a threshold. 28 meaning units, sorted into three themes.
  • Phase 4, design translation Themes became sketches, then AI-assisted visual interpretations (used only as a way to make rough ideas comparable, not as design output), then CAD models of two concepts: a modified front wheel for threshold crossing, and a cushion-lift mechanism for seat height. Both were 3D printed and tested as part of a VR home environment in Unity, run on a Meta Quest, with touch-based artifacts standing in for the physical wheel and seat changes. Fed back to participants for a third round of feedback.

What changed through participation

Three directions, in order of how much they were talked about.

  • Threshold crossing showed up constantly. Small floor-level changes (a bathroom step, a raised doorsill) were where independence broke down and where people needed help from someone else. The front-wheel concept responds to that directly.
  • Height and reach mattered as much as movement itself. Getting into the kitchen isn't useful if you still can't reach the platform. Participants connected wheelchair height to bed transfers, cooking, and how their feet sat against the footrest.
  • Affordability and modularity ran under everything. Nobody pushed back on the idea of a better wheelchair. They pushed back on the idea of an expensive one. The preference was consistently for attachable add-ons to an existing chair over a complete robotic replacement.

    What didn't work

    The analysis translated everyday mobility barriers into design components. These were not final medical products. They were ways to think through access, adaptation, and assistive mobility in low-resource domestic settings.

    • The booklet's speculative section, meant to prompt future-facing ideas, mostly produced criticism instead. Cost, repair, and realism for Indian homes came up before anything generative did. Not a failure exactly, but a sign that static, image-based prompts aren't the right format for getting people to imagine rather than evaluate.
    • AI-generated visuals were useful only when tightly constrained. Left alone, they drifted toward sleek, expensive-looking, robotic concepts that had nothing to do with what participants actually said they needed. They worked as a way to compare rough sketches quickly, not as a source of ideas.

    Translation from Hindi to English (all interviews were conducted in Hindi) introduced some loss, mostly around tone and hesitation rather than fact.

    The aim was not to make one perfect assistive device. The aim was to make domestic mobility barriers visible enough to be discussed, critiqued, and designed from.

    What got built

    The project developed a set of speculative and functional prototypes. Each one responds to a specific domestic friction that appeared during the research.

    • Expandable front wheel - a wheel concept for thresholds and uneven surfaces.
    • Inflatable cushion - a soft height-adjustment idea for kitchen and counter-level tasks.
    • Height-changing seat concepts - scissor-lift and gas-spring explorations for transfers.
    • Scoop mudguard and tread ideas - add-on concepts for Indian roads, pavements, and domestic edges.
    • VR domestic simulation - a walkthrough for testing concepts inside a home-like environment.

    The thesis isn't trying to ship a wheelchair. It's trying to show that you can move design conversations about disability earlier, before the prototype stage, without losing the user along the way. The bigger argument is about timing. Most assistive technology research brings users in at the end, as testers of something already built. This project tried the opposite: build almost nothing until the people who'd use it had already shaped the problem.

    DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

    01 / 03

    What I learned

    • Domestic accessibility is interactional. It is shaped by the relation between body, device, space, surface, caregiver, and routine.
    • Assistive technology abandonment starts early. It often begins before the device reaches the user, when design decisions are made without situated participation.
    • Participation is not just feedback. It can redirect the research question, reveal hidden constraints, and surface design criteria that would otherwise stay invisible.
    • Speculative artifacts help people speak. Booklets, animations, and prototypes made it easier for participants to critique future possibilities instead of only describing past difficulties.
    • Evaluation must include body diversity. Questions of weight, posture, measurement, strength, fatigue, and comfort are not secondary details. They are central to assistive mobility design.

    Where it points

    This thesis points toward a more situated approach to assistive technology design — one where homes are treated as testing grounds, participants are treated as knowledge holders, and prototypes become tools for dialogue.

    The next step is deeper co-design, mechanical testing, home-based evaluation, and collaboration across HCI, disability studies, rehabilitation, product design, and assistive technology engineering.

    CONCLUSION / OUTPUTS

    01 / 03

    Discussion

    • The concepts functioned as artefacts, not final solutions. Participants connected them to lived domestic problems - which confirms the research direction is grounded, but mechanical, ergonomic, and in-home testing remain necessary before any of these directions can be treated as resolved.
    • This evaluation was never about validating a product. It was about confirming that the design questions being asked are the right ones - and it did that. The next stage inherits a clearer set of technical problems, not open-ended uncertainty.
    • What the testing revealed is a gap between conceptual relevance and functional readiness. The ideas resonate. The engineering does not yet exist to support them. That gap is the most honest finding this stage could produce.
    • Co-speculation and Particiatory design at this scale earns its value by surfacing the questions that structured lab testing cannot ask. These concepts did that. The path forward is not to defend them as solutions but to use them as the basis for the harder, more specific work ahead.
