● ARCHIVED GRADUATE RESEARCH INTERNSHIP MARINE HMI

Human Factors–Driven Cockpit for Indian Ferries

A graduate research internship exploring how Indian ferry cockpits can be redesigned around reach, visibility, cognitive load, and future autonomy.

ROLE

Graduate Research Internship · IDC IIT Bombay · 2025

FOCUS

Human Factors · Marine HMI · Indian Anthropometry

METHODS

Field Visit · Stakeholder Interviews · Task Mapping · Standards Review · Card Sorting · CAD · Full-scale Mock-up Testing

OUTPUT

Integrated helm architecture · Digital dashboard IA · Physical prototype · Evaluation guidelines
HUMAN FACTORS MARINE HMI PARTICIPATORY METHODS SAFETY-CRITICAL INTERFACE PHYSICAL-DIGITAL DESIGN

Can a ferry cockpit be designed around the operator’s body before its hardware?

This project began with a simple discomfort: the boats were modern, but the operator interface still behaved like a collection of added parts. Screens, switches, alarms, throttle, wheel and dashboard information were present, but not held together by one human-factors logic.

I treated the helm not as a console, but as a physical-digital interaction space.

The problem

  • Existing ferry helms often carry too many buttons and screens in a small area, creating cognitive load during docking, departure and emergency response.
  • The Mumbai field visit surfaced a very practical problem: captains and technicians described confusion in real situations, difficulty training new joinees, poor button hierarchy and a cockpit language shaped around a narrow body type.
  • Technicians also pointed to interface and maintenance issues: colour coding was not always meaningful, text could become hard to read, and wiring/control organisation was not always structurally clean.
  • The larger design issue was not one bad button. It was too much information placed in too little space without clear priority.

What I set out to do

The aim was to redesign the physical helm and the digital dashboard as one integrated cockpit for Indian inland ferry contexts. Three layers had to work together: the reach-zone architecture of the physical console, the information architecture of the dashboard, and the decision about what stays physical, what moves digital, and what needs redundancy.

Not a screen redesign. Not a form redesign. A body-first cockpit system.

Field grounding

MEETING 01

Mumbai ferry dock visit

Field visit with ferry technicians and two captains. Observed a similar cockpit ecology: dense controls, layered screens, and limited hierarchy.

THEMATIC ANALYSIS

Button load and readability

Field notes were coded into recurring themes: control overload, poor readability, weak colour logic, training difficulty, and male-body-centric cockpit assumptions.

PHASE 02 METHOD

Participatory task-mapping workshop

Planned with Kochi skippers around cruising, docking, emergency handling, and system checks. The aim was to map what operators touch, watch, trust, ignore, or need duplicated.

WHY IT MATTERS

From cockpit to care

The project sits outside disability design, but asks a similar question. How can safety-critical systems fit diverse bodies instead of forcing bodies to adapt?

The participatory cockpit mapping game was adapted from Brandt and Messeter’s design games approach , where structured cards and scenario prompts help stakeholders externalise work practices, negotiate priorities, and build a shared understanding of the design problem.

PROCESS / PARTICIPATORY TASK MAPPING

01 / 03

The framework

Three structural decisions shaped the project: reach zoning, function allocation, and dashboard information architecture.

01

Reach zoning

Primary, secondary and tertiary reach zones were mapped around Indian 5th-percentile female to 95th-percentile male body dimensions. Safety-critical and high-frequency actions had to stay close.

02

Function allocation

Steering, throttle, horn and emergency stop remained physical. Monitoring, logs and settings moved digital. Navigation status and key alarms were mirrored for redundancy.

03

Dashboard IA

Card sorting and function grouping led to a shallow tab structure: Home, GPS / Navigation, Buttons and Settings. The goal was not more data, but fewer decisions under pressure.

Physical vs digital decisions

PHYSICAL

Steering · throttle · horn · E-stop · critical lights

DIGITAL

Engine monitoring · logs · settings · environmental data · non-critical alerts

MIRRORED

Navigation status · major alarms · safety feedback

The question was not “can this be on a screen?” It was “should the operator need to look away to do this?”

Building it

The project moved from hand sketches to Rhino CAD, 3D-printed models, full-scale mock-up and reach/posture evaluation. The form was treated as an ergonomic system: wheel height, throttle grip height, display band, knee clearance and console depth had to work together.

Dimension Target / Recommended Design
Wheel centre height 900–1,000 mm 920 mm
Top of main display 1,050–1,200 mm 990 mm
Throttle grip height 800–900 mm 810 mm
Console width 900–1,300 mm 1,200 mm
Primary reach radius 350–450 mm 420 mm

SKETCH / CAD / MOCK-UP / PROTOTYPE

01 / 04

The dashboard

The digital dashboard used a dark interface for night operations and glare reduction, with a restricted colour palette: neutral for normal, amber for caution and red for alarm. The aim was to reduce visual noise and make safety states readable without interpretation.

HOME

Battery, RPM, fuel and essential status in one scan.

GPS

Chart view, forward camera and vessel orientation for docking awareness.

BUTTONS

Grouped vessel systems: lights, pumps, thrusters, VHF and autopilot.

SETTINGS

Low-frequency configuration: units, brightness and system preferences.

Evaluation

The physical prototype and digital prototype were tested together because a dashboard that works on a laptop can still fail inside a cockpit. Participants moved between sitting and standing, reached for controls, checked screen readability and completed dashboard tasks such as checking fuel, switching on navigation lights and acknowledging an alarm.

REACH

Primary controls stayed within workable reach, with minor changes needed for wheel distance and knee clearance.

IA

Home and GPS tasks were completed with low hesitation. Alarm grouping on the Buttons screen needed clearer separation.

LIMIT

The test was static and lab-based. It did not include vibration, rain, real glare, motion or operational time pressure.

The design was directionally validated, not operationally certified.

What I learned

  • Clutter is structural. A helm becomes cluttered when every new system is added as a separate layer.
  • Physical and digital cannot be designed separately. The operator experiences them as one cockpit, not as two design problems.
  • Indian anthropometry is not a detail. Body data changes who can reach, see, sit, stand and work safely.
  • Participatory methods matter even in technical systems. Skippers do not only use controls. They develop routines, shortcuts, warnings and embodied knowledge that a dashboard cannot reveal alone.

Where it points

The next step is in-situ testing on an operational ferry with real captains under docking, glare, rain, vibration and time pressure. The broader method is transferable: anthropometric reach zoning, task-driven function allocation and card-sort-informed dashboard IA can support other Indian inland ferries, coastal craft and small workboats.

The longer research direction is a cockpit that can move between manual, assisted and remote modes without forcing the operator to rebuild their mental model each time.

A future ferry cockpit should not be more digital by default. It should be more legible to the body using it.
